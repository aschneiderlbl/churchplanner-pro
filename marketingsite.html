How we protect your church’s data Security

Our production environment is hosted on AWS EC2 and protected with multiple layers of security. ChurchPlannerPro is designed with security as a foundational principle.

Network

  • The application is delivered through a Cloudflare Tunnel, so the server is not exposed directly to the public internet.
  • AWS firewall rules (Security Groups) are configured to restrict inbound access.
  • SSH access is limited to explicitly allowed sources.
  • Application and database ports are bound to localhost and are not publicly accessible.

Access management

  • Administrative access to the server is controlled through SSH key authentication and operating-system permissions.
  • Cloudflare Tunnel configuration is restricted to authorized Cloudflare accounts.
  • Application secrets (API keys, database credentials) are stored outside of container images and protected with restricted file permissions.

Threat protection

  • Cloudflare provides edge protection, including DDoS mitigation.
  • Additional controls such as rate limiting and firewall rules can be applied.
  • The server is monitored and can be configured with alerts for availability and health.

Information protection

  • Data in transit is encrypted using TLS/HTTPS via Cloudflare.
  • The tunnel connection between Cloudflare and the server is encrypted.
  • Data at rest is stored on encrypted AWS storage volumes.

Backups

  • Nightly backups of the database can be produced and retained for a defined period.
  • Backups can optionally be copied to an external storage location for added resilience.

Planning Center integrations: connecting the rest of your church tools

September 22, 2026 2 min read

Planning Center is a strong tool, and many churches build around it. But most churches still run 7 or more tools: giving through Pushpay or Stripe, accounting in QuickBooks, email in Google or Microsoft, and spreadsheets for the rest. This post covers where the gaps usually are and how to close them.

Where the gaps show up

  • Giving to accounting. Deposits land in one system and the books in another. Someone reconciles them by hand each month.
  • Members across systems. A new family registers for VBS, gives online, and signs up to volunteer. That can mean three records for one household.
  • Volunteers outside Planning Center. Some teams still use SignUpGenius or a spreadsheet, so schedules live in two places.
  • Reporting. Budget vs. actual, attendance, and giving trends need data from several tools at once.

What to look for in an integration

  1. One clean member record. The integration should match people across systems and flag duplicates, not add more.
  2. Your tools stay in place. You shouldn't have to give up Planning Center or your giving platform to connect them.
  3. The next step happens on its own. Connecting data is step one. The payoff is automation: a gift updates the record, a new member gets a welcome, a dropped shift gets filled.

Church management software for small churches: getting off spreadsheets

September 22, 2026 2 min read

Plenty of small churches run on a shared spreadsheet, a paper signup sheet in the narthex, and a weekly email. For a while, that's fine. This post covers when it stops being fine, what to look for in software, and how to switch without a big project.

Signs you've outgrown spreadsheets

  • Two people keep two versions of the member list.
  • Volunteer gaps show up on Sunday morning, not Wednesday.
  • Year-end giving statements take a full weekend.
  • Only one person knows how anything works, and they're a volunteer.

If two or more of these sound familiar, the manual system is costing more than it saves.

What small churches should look for

  1. A price that fits. Free church management software often limits the features you'll need, like giving statements or volunteer scheduling. Look for flat, low pricing that includes everything.
  2. Help moving your data in. Your spreadsheets are your records. The right tool imports them and flags duplicates and missing fields before you start.
  3. Outcomes, not modules. You don't need 40 features. You need volunteer shifts filled, statements out on time, and programs and members kept up to date.
  4. Help when no one is on staff. If you don't have an administrator, look for a provider that offers people, not just software.

How to switch without a big project

  1. Start with one list, usually members.
  2. Clean it up: duplicates, missing emails, outdated households.
  3. Add the next area only once the first one is working, often volunteers or giving.
  4. Retire each spreadsheet once its replacement is in use.

Church administration without a full-time administrator

September 22, 2026 2 min read

Most small and mid-sized churches have software. What many don't have is a person whose job is to run it. So church administration falls to the pastor, a volunteer, or a part-time staffer who already has another role.

This post covers what church administration actually includes, why it breaks down without an owner, and three ways to cover it.

What church administration includes

A church administrator's job description usually spans five areas:

  • Members: new members, address changes, deceased members, sacrament records.
  • Giving and finance: reconciling deposits, tracking budget against actual, sending giving statements.
  • Volunteers: building schedules, filling open shifts, handling swaps. (More on volunteer scheduling)
  • Programs: Sunday school and VBS registration, nametags, rooms.
  • Communication: the newsletter, event announcements, member questions. (More on church communication)

None of these is hard on its own. The work piles up because each one lives in a different tool.

Why it breaks down without an owner

The typical church runs 7 or more tools: one for giving, one for member records, a signup tool for volunteers, and spreadsheets for everything else. Someone has to carry information between them. When no one owns that job, the same problems show up:

  • Duplicate or outdated member records.
  • Giving statements that go out late or with errors.
  • Volunteer gaps nobody sees until Sunday morning.
  • The pastor doing data entry on a Tuesday night.

Three ways to cover church administration

  1. Connect the tools you already have. Connecting your giving, member, and finance tools means records match without re-typing. Start by cleaning up duplicates so every system works from the same data.
  2. Automate what happens next. A new member joins, a volunteer drops a shift, a donation comes in. Each of these should trigger the next step on its own: a welcome, a reminder, a record update.
  3. Add part-time admin help. Some work still needs a person. A part-time administrator who already knows the system can handle reconciliation, statements, and scheduling in a few hours a week.
